Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Federal Central Tax Office (Bundeszentralamt für Steuern - BZSt) together with the competent local Finanzamt (Tax Office) and ELSTER (Elektronische Steuererklärung) online portal; official portal: www.elster.de and www.bzst.de

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a German GmbH registers with the Commercial Register (Handelsregister) via a notary, then obtains a tax number (Steuernummer) from the local Finanzamt after submitting the registration form (Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung) via ELSTER or in paper form. The GmbH also applies for a VAT ID (Umsatzsteuer-Identifikationsnummer) from BZSt using the official application portal (BZSt-USt-IdNr.-Portal). The notary's certified incorporation filing, German bank account, and managing director registration (Geschäftsführer) are prerequisite steps.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A foreign entity establishing a German GmbH registers with the Commercial Register (Handelsregister) via a notary, then obtains a tax number (Steuernummer) from the local Finanzamt after submitting the registration form (Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung) via ELSTER or in paper form. The GmbH also applies for a VAT ID (Umsatzsteuer-Identifikationsnummer) from BZSt using the official application portal (BZSt-USt-IdNr.-Portal). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Germany applies entity-level taxation for GmbH with no check-the-box election. A GmbH cannot be treated as a pass-through for German income tax. However, certain hybrid mismatch elections can apply under EU Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive (ATAD) implementation (Sec. 4k EStG) and sections of the German Corporate Tax Act (KStG) to restrict or align treatment of hybrid instruments and hybrid entities. An Option for transparent treatment under § 1a KStG may be available only for domestic partnerships, not for a GmbH. A GmbH may elect group taxation (Organschaft) with a controlling enterprise under Sec. 14 KStG, which consolidates income and VAT for qualifying profit-and-loss transfer agreements.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

19%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No general VAT registration threshold for German resident entities: any GmbH engaged in commercial or professional activities must register for VAT from inception, regardless of turnover. A small-business exception (Kleinunternehmer § 19 UStG) applies only to natural persons and certain unincorporated businesses, not to a GmbH, and the EU distance sales threshold of €10,000 applies only to non-resident suppliers of intra-Community distance sales of goods.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Export of goods to third countries (outside EU) is zero-rated under § 6 UStG if evidence of actual physical export is provided. Intra-Community supplies to another EU country are zero-rated under § 6a UStG only if the purchaser provides a valid foreign VAT ID, the goods move to another Member State, and summary declaration (Intrastat) requirements are met. Services connected with export goods and certain cross-border B2B services, including data processing, copyright/licensing and digital services to non-resident entrepreneurs, are zero-rated or subject to reverse charge under §§ 7, 8, 13b UStG. For AI subtitle/dubbing services: for exports of digital deliverables to non-EU customers, German VAT generally follows B2B reverse charge (customer accounts for VAT) or B2C place-of-supply rules; zero rating for directly held export goods requires physical movement of media/substrate, not pure intangible digital delivery.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Ausfuhr- und Abfertigungsbescheinigung / electronic ATLAS export declaration)
  • Commercial Invoice (Handelsrechnung)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (Konnossement / Luftfrachtbrief)
  • Proof of physical export (e.g., export certificate / Ausfuhrbescheinigung)

Germany introduced mandatory e-invoicing (E-Rechnung) for all domestic B2B transactions from 1 January 2025: every German-resident supplier must issue structured e-invoices conforming to EN 16931 (XRechnung or ZUGFeRD) via interoperable transmission, but no real-time fiscal clearance/per-transaction tax authority stamping is currently required. The BZSt receives VAT return data electronically via ELSTER/VDA; from 2024 there is an obligation to report cross-border transactions in the Transaction Network (CTN/ITSS) implemented under the DAC7 Directive. Specific API reporting obligations apply to digital platform sellers (e.g., Stripe/Wise) under the Platform Tax Transparency Act (PStTG), requiring annual disclosure of seller data to BZSt, but there is no immediate per-invoice fiscal clearance API for ordinary GmbH issuance.

Tax authorities require export evidence to be maintained for at least 10 years; missing evidence causes VAT assessment at 19% with interest (6% per annum) and potential late payment penalties (Säumniszuschlag). Intentional or fraudulent zero-rating claims constitute tax evasion (Steuerhinterziehung) under § 370 AO, punishable with imprisonment up to 5 years, fines, and full retrospective clawback of input VAT deductions plus interest. Negligent non-disclosure risks fines under § 378 AO. Export shipments lacking customs release (ATLAS) or supporting transport documents are retrospectively recharacterized as taxable domestic supplies.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Germany provides full de jure and de facto convertibility on both the current account and the capital account. Article 63 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) and Section 3(1) AWG guarantee unrestricted cross-border payments and capital movements with third countries, subject only to EU sanctions, anti-money-laundering controls, and mandatory statistical reporting. No exchange-control licence or prior approval is required for inbound equity, subscription revenue, royalty inflows, profit remittances, dividend distributions, or loan repayments. The practical profit-remittance hurdles for a DE_GMBH are: (a) the statutory German dividend withholding tax of 25% plus 5.5% solidarity surcharge, producing an aggregate 26.375% rate, which may be reduced to 0% for qualifying EU corporate shareholders under the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive or partially mitigated under an applicable Double Tax Treaty for third-country investors; (b) ordinary corporate income tax (Körperschaftsteuer at 15% plus solidarity surcharge, i.e., 15.825%) and municipal trade tax (Gewerbesteuer levied at local rates between roughly 7% and 17%); and (c) the AWV balance-of-payments reporting duty for every cross-border payment or receipt exceeding EUR 12,500, which is post-transaction and does not block settlement. Revenues generated in EUR, USD, GBP, or CNY may be freely retained in any currency and remitted abroad as royalties, licence fees, dividends, or intercompany service payments after corporate-law formalities and tax filings are completed.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Germany imposes no export-proceeds surrender, conversion, or retention regime. There is no statutory timeline obligating the conversion of foreign-currency export receipts into EUR, and no mandatory conversion ratio applies to the DE_GMBH's subtitle-transcription, AI voice-dubbing, or post-production licensing proceeds. Export earnings may legally be held indefinitely in offshore or onshore multi-currency accounts. The only binding obligation is statistical: any inward payment from a non-resident exceeding EUR 12,500 (or foreign-currency equivalent) must be reported to the Deutsche Bundesbank no later than the fifth working day of the month following the value date, using the Bundesbank's electronic reporting channel and the correct Z3/Z2 service category. This requirement is purely administrative; it contains no surrender duty, no minimum retention rule, and no forced hedging mandate under German law.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank (the central bank of the Federal Republic of Germany), serving concurrently as the foreign-exchange and cross-border payment reporting administration under the German Foreign Trade and Payments Act (Außenwirtschaftsgesetz – AWG) and the Foreign Trade and Payments Ordinance (Außenwirtschaftsverordnung – AWV), with the Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Climate Action (Bundesministerium für Wirtschaft und Klimaschutz – BMWK) as the competent policy and licensing authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High as a supplementary collections rail for global PSP receipts (Stripe, Wise Business, Payoneer); Low as the primary or sole treasury spine for the GmbH. Legal constraints under the German Banking Act (Kreditwesengesetz – KWG) and the EU Payment Services Directive (PSD2): (1) an offshore EMI may not lawfully accept funds as a deposit in Germany; only a BaFin-authorised credit institution holds a deposit-taking licence under KWG Section 1(1) no. 1; (2) any non-EU EMI providing payment services to a German GmbH must be passported or separately licensed by BaFin under Section 63 KWG and must comply with the German Anti-Money Laundering Act (Geldwäschegesetz – GwG), including sanctions screening and transaction monitoring; (3) an EMI cannot issue the bank certificate evidencing payment of the statutory minimum capital (EUR 25,000) – only a licensed German bank can provide the Kapitalaufbringungsbestätigung required for the Handelsregister filing; (4) e-money balances held at an EMI are not protected by the German statutory deposit-guarantee scheme (Einlagensicherungsgesetz) and in an EMI insolvency would rank as safeguarded funds under the E-Money Directive, creating counterparty risk; and (5) EMI accounts are operationally valuable but should be swept daily into the tier-1 German settlement account to maintain clean audit trails, proper VAT documentation, and insolvency remoteness.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium-High as a supplementary collections rail for global PSP receipts (Stripe, Wise Business, Payoneer); Low as the primary or sole treasury spine for the GmbH. Legal constraints under the German Banking Act (Kreditwesengesetz – KWG) and the EU Payment Services Directive (PSD2): (1) an offshore EMI may not lawfully accept funds as a deposit in Germany; only a BaFin-authorised credit institution holds a deposit-taking licence under KWG Section 1(1) no. 1; (2) any non-EU EMI providing payment services to a German GmbH must be passported or separately licensed by BaFin under Section 63 KWG and must comply with the German Anti-Money Laundering Act (Geldwäschegesetz – GwG), including sanctions screening and transaction monitoring; (3) an EMI cannot issue the bank certificate evidencing payment of the statutory minimum capital (EUR 25,000) – only a licensed German bank can provide the Kapitalaufbringungsbestätigung required for the Handelsregister filing; (4) e-money balances held at an EMI are not protected by the German statutory deposit-guarantee scheme (Einlagensicherungsgesetz) and in an EMI insolvency would rank as safeguarded funds under the E-Money Directive, creating counterparty risk; and (5) EMI accounts are operationally valuable but should be swept daily into the tier-1 German settlement account to maintain clean audit trails, proper VAT documentation, and insolvency remoteness.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% corporation tax, plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the corporation tax, producing an effective corporation-tax burden of 15.825%; municipal trade tax generally produces a combined effective corporate burden of approximately 23% to 33%, depending on the municipal multiplier.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% German capital-yield withholding tax plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the withholding tax, producing a combined statutory burden of 26.375%, before treaty, EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive or domestic relief.

The standard deadline is 31 July of the following calendar year. Where a tax adviser files the return, the general extended deadline is the last day of February of the second following calendar year, subject to statutory exceptions, tax-office requests and special transitional rules.

Withholding tax is generally due and payable to the tax office by the 10th day after the end of the month in which the taxable capital income was paid or credited.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For a German taxpayer with cross-border related-party transactions, a Local File is generally required where the annual value of supplies of goods with a related party exceeds EUR 6 million or the annual value of other transactions, including services, financing and royalties, exceeds EUR 600,000. A Master File is generally required for a German entity belonging to a multinational group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 100 million where the German entity engages in relevant cross-border transactions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Germany has no separate general economic-substance-regulations regime equivalent to certain low-tax jurisdictions. Substance remains material under German transfer-pricing rules, the Foreign Tax Act, general anti-abuse rules, beneficial-ownership analysis, treaty anti-abuse provisions, EU directive relief, CFC rules and the principal-purpose test in applicable treaties. Relevant evidence includes German personnel, decision-making authority, premises, functions, assets, risks, financing capacity, commercial purpose and actual performance of services. A conduit company, contractual intermediary or nominal IP owner may be denied treaty or directive benefits and may face transfer-pricing adjustments.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Under Section 370 of the German Fiscal Code, tax evasion is punishable by imprisonment of up to five years or a criminal fine. Particularly serious cases may attract imprisonment from six months to ten years. Unpaid tax, interest and procedural assessments remain recoverable.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may incur personal civil liability for tax amounts not paid because of intentional or grossly negligent breach of tax duties under Section 69 of the Fiscal Code. Criminal prosecution may result in imprisonment, fines and asset confiscation. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may occur through arrest warrants, bail conditions or enforcement measures.
Late filing and inaccurate corporation-tax or trade-tax returns

Statutory sanction: Late-filing surcharges, tax assessments based on estimates, tax interest, enforcement costs and administrative fines may apply. Material deliberate misstatements may be prosecuted as tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors must organize timely and accurate tax compliance. Personal liability may arise for avoidable tax losses caused by intentional or grossly negligent failure to supervise filing, payment and recordkeeping.
Dividend, royalty or interest withholding-tax failure

Statutory sanction: The GmbH may be assessed for the unpaid withholding tax, solidarity surcharge, interest and penalties. Intentional non-withholding may constitute tax evasion or aiding tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be personally liable for withholding-tax losses under Section 69 of the Fiscal Code. Criminal exposure depends on knowledge, intent and the factual role in the distribution or reporting failure.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and undocumented related-party transactions

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may make income adjustments, deny deductions, impose withholding tax, assess interest and impose documentation-related penalties. A failure to produce records may lead to adverse estimation and surcharges generally linked to the adjustment.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability where deliberate or grossly negligent transfer-pricing conduct causes unpaid German tax. Criminal exposure arises where fabricated records or intentional concealment establishes tax evasion.
Treaty abuse, conduit arrangements and lack of beneficial ownership

Statutory sanction: Treaty or directive relief may be denied, statutory withholding tax and interest may be assessed, and anti-abuse rules may apply. Fraudulent claims can result in administrative or criminal tax sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve sham arrangements may face civil tax liability, criminal prosecution and confiscation. Travel restrictions are fact-specific and not an automatic consequence.
Beneficial-owner register and anti-money-laundering violations

Statutory sanction: Violations of the German Anti-Money Laundering Act may attract administrative fines, in serious cases up to EUR 1 million or a statutory multiple of the economic benefit, with additional publication and supervisory measures where applicable. Money laundering under the Criminal Code may result in imprisonment or fines.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and compliance officers may face administrative fines and criminal liability where they intentionally or negligently participate in concealment, laundering or false ownership reporting. Asset freezing and confiscation may apply; travel bans are case-specific.
Insolvency delay and creditor prejudice

Statutory sanction: Failure to file for insolvency within the statutory period may constitute a criminal offense under Section 15a of the Insolvency Code, generally punishable by imprisonment of up to three years or a fine, with negligent conduct subject to lower sanctions. Repayment and director-liability claims may also arise.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be personally liable for prohibited post-insolvency payments, creditor losses, unpaid taxes and social-security contributions. Criminal proceedings may involve asset seizure, confiscation and, where legally justified, arrest or travel restrictions.
Payroll tax and social-security non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Back taxes, social-security contributions, interest, late-payment surcharges, penalties and criminal sanctions may apply. Deliberate withholding of employee social-security contributions may constitute a criminal offense.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid wage tax and social-security contributions where statutory management duties were breached. Imprisonment or fines may apply for deliberate conduct.
Artificial intelligence, copyright and data-protection non-compliance with tax consequences

Statutory sanction: Primary consequences may include copyright injunctions, damages, contractual claims and data-protection fines. Unsupported payments may be denied as deductions, recharacterized as royalties or distributions, and subjected to withholding tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability for deliberate or negligent corporate misconduct and personal exposure where they knowingly approve unlawful exploitation or fraudulent tax characterization. Criminal liability depends on the specific copyright, data-protection, fraud or tax offense.
